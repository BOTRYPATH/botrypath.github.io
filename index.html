Project context

Understanding a major plant pathogen to develop faster and more targeted tools

Plant-pathogenic fungi are a major challenge for crop protection. At the same time, the use of fungicides is increasingly constrained, reinforcing the need for better knowledge of pathogens and for faster tools to identify and characterize them.

Botrytis cinerea, the causal agent of grey mould, is a major model in phytopathology. Its genome is known and many molecular tools are available, yet important aspects of its biology and of the physical properties of its spores remain poorly understood.

MAP — biological expertise

The MAP laboratory contributes its expertise in fungal genomics, virulence and mutant production. In particular, it provides wild-type and cell-wall mutant strains needed to link biophysical signatures with fungal biology.

Microbiologie, Adaptation et Pathogénie

MAP studies fungal plant pathogens, including B. cinerea, and provides the biological models required to evaluate the microfluidic tools developed in BOTRYPATH.

The scientific challenge

Why does genetic purification take so long?

After genetic modification, a B. cinerea spore can contain several nuclei, and the mutation may not be present in all of them. This makes it necessary to isolate and regrow individual spores until genetically homogeneous material is obtained.

The challenge is therefore twofold:

  1. Among the genetically modified spores, rapidly isolate those containing a single nucleus in order to accelerate the production of genetically pure mutant colonies.
  2. Develop new label-free biophysical measurements capable of probing fungal spores beyond conventional biological observations.

This is where microfluidic technology becomes an invaluable tool.

Why microfluidics?

Lab-on-a-chip technologies bring physics into fungal biology

Microfluidic lab-on-a-chip systems are particularly well suited to the manipulation and characterization of individual cells. They make it possible to exploit intrinsic physical properties—such as size, mechanical response or electrical impedance—without necessarily adding labels that could interfere with the biological sample.

In BOTRYPATH, this creates a natural bridge between the technological know-how of INL and the biological expertise of MAP: INL develops microfluidic functions and microsystems, while MAP provides the fungal strains, biological questions and reference characterizations needed to interpret the measurements.
